Reading group copy · Issue XXIX · marked up by J.R., A.M., and T.S.

Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures for World Models

Abstract

Most learned world models are trained to predict pixels: given a frame and an action, produce the next frame. We argue this objective wastes capacity on unpredictable, task-irrelevant detail — the exact texture of a leaf, the grain of static on a screen — and propose instead that a world model should predict in a learned representation space. A joint-embedding predictive architecture (JEPA) trains an encoder and a predictor jointly: the encoder maps observations to a latent space, and the predictor forecasts future latents from past ones and an action, without ever reconstructing pixels.1 We show this shifts model capacity toward the regularities that matter for control and planning.

§1 · Introduction

A model of the world is only useful if it can be queried: what happens if I push this block? Where will the ball be in two seconds? Reconstruction-based world models answer these questions by generating a full future frame, then letting a downstream policy read out whatever it needs. This is expensive, and worse, it forces the model to spend most of its capacity on the parts of the scene that carry no information about the outcome the policy actually cares about — the color of a distant wall, the shape of a shadow.

The joint-embedding approach we describe here follows a simpler principle: predict only in a space where prediction is possible. An encoder learns a representation in which the future is more predictable than it is in pixel space, precisely because that representation is free to discard whatever cannot be predicted. This is not a new idea in isolation — self-supervised representation learning has used joint embeddings for years — but applying it to a predictive, action-conditioned setting for world modeling is a more recent turn, most clearly stated in LeCun's 2022 position paper on autonomous machine intelligence.[1]

§2 · Method

The architecture has three pieces: an encoder, a predictor, and a target encoder. The encoder maps the current observation to a latent vector. The predictor takes that latent, together with the action taken, and forecasts the latent of the next observation. The target encoder — an exponential moving average of the main encoder's weights — produces the "true" future latent that the predictor is trained to match. Using a separate, slowly-updated target encoder is what prevents the whole system from collapsing to a trivial constant representation, since the predictor's target keeps drifting slightly out of reach of a lazy shortcut.

Training proceeds entirely without pixel reconstruction. The loss is a distance between the predicted latent and the target latent, plus a regularization term that keeps the latent distribution from collapsing in variance — an isotropic covariance penalty, applied per batch.2 We found this regularizer to be the single most sensitive hyperparameter in the whole setup; too weak, and the encoder finds a shortcut within a few thousand steps; too strong, and useful structure gets pushed out of the latent along with the noise.

§3 · Results & Discussion

Across the manipulation and navigation tasks in our test suite, the joint-embedding predictor reached comparable downstream task performance to a pixel-reconstruction baseline using roughly one-twentieth of the parameters,3 and trained in a third of the wall-clock time. The gap was largest in visually cluttered scenes,[3] exactly where we expected the reconstruction objective to waste the most capacity on irrelevant detail.

The regularizer's sensitivity deserves more attention than we can give it here — see the production schedule for this issue for the reviewer thread tracking that exact question.[2] We also suspect the target-encoder update rate interacts with task horizon in ways our current experiments do not cleanly separate; a natural next step is a sweep across both.

"The interesting failures were never in the predictor. They were always a symptom of a collapsing encoder, dressed up as something else."

None of this is a claim that pixels are the wrong representation for every purpose — a model built to generate images for a human to look at still needs to predict pixels eventually. Our claim is narrower: for a model whose only job is to support planning and control, prediction in a learned latent space is a better use of a fixed parameter budget than prediction in pixel space.
